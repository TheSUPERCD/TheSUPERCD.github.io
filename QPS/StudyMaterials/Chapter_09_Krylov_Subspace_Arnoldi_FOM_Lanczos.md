## 1. Krylov Subspaces and Their Fundamental Properties

Krylov subspace methods represent one of the top algorithmic developments in modern scientific computing. Instead of searching along single-variable directions (like Steepest Descent), they construct an expanding sequence of optimal polynomial approximation spaces.

### 1.1 Mathematical Definition
Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be a real matrix, and let $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a non-zero vector.
The **$m$-th Krylov Subspace** $\mathcal{K}_m(A, v)$ generated by $A$ and $v$ is the subspace spanned by the Krylov sequence:
$$\mathbf{\mathcal{K}_m(A, v) = \text{span}\{v, A v, A^2 v, A^3 v, \dots, A^{m-1} v\}}$$

In polynomial notation, $\mathcal{K}_m(A, v)$ is the set of all vectors obtained by applying a polynomial in $A$ of degree at most $m-1$ to vector $v$:
$$\mathbf{\mathcal{K}_m(A, v) = \{ p(A) v \mid p \in \mathcal{P}_{m-1} \}}$$
where $\mathcal{P}_{m-1}$ is the space of real polynomials of degree $\le m-1$.

### 1.2 Motivation for Linear Systems ($Ax = b$)
When solving $Ax = b$ starting from initial guess $x_0$ with initial residual $r_0 = b - Ax_0$, we choose the generating vector to be the initial residual $v = r_0$:
$$\mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0) = \text{span}\{r_0, A r_0, A^2 r_0, \dots, A^{m-1} r_0\}$$
If candidate solutions are drawn from the affine subspace $x_m \in x_0 + \mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$, then:
$$x_m = x_0 + q_{m-1}(A) r_0 \quad \text{for some } q_{m-1} \in \mathcal{P}_{m-1}$$
The corresponding residual is:
$$r_m = b - A x_m = b - A (x_0 + q_{m-1}(A) r_0) = (b - A x_0) - A q_{m-1}(A) r_0$$
$$\mathbf{r_m = [I - A q_{m-1}(A)] r_0 = p_m(A) r_0}$$
where $p_m(t) = 1 - t q_{m-1}(t)$ is a polynomial of degree $m$ satisfying the crucial constraint:
$$\mathbf{p_m(0) = 1}$$
**Core Insight**: Krylov methods solve $Ax = b$ by finding an optimal polynomial $p_m(t)$ that satisfies $p_m(0) = 1$ while making the residual vector $r_m = p_m(A) r_0$ as small as possible!

### 1.3 Minimal Polynomial, Grade, and Invariance
- **Grade of $v$ with respect to $A$ ($\nu(v, A)$)**: The smallest positive integer $m$ such that the vectors $\{v, Av, \dots, A^m v\}$ are linearly dependent.
- **Invariance**: At step $m = \nu$, the Krylov subspace becomes **invariant under $A$**:
  $$A \mathcal{K}_m(A, v) \subseteq \mathcal{K}_m(A, v)$$
- **Cayley-Hamilton Theorem**: Every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation:
  $$P_n(A) = \det(A - \lambda I) = \mathbf{0}$$
  The minimal polynomial $q(t)$ is the monic polynomial of least degree such that $q(A) = \mathbf{0}$.
- **Finite Termination Theorem**: If $m = \nu(r_0, A)$, the exact solution $x^* = A^{-1} b$ is contained **strictly inside** the affine space $x_0 + \mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$. The exact solution is found in at most $m \le n$ steps!

## 2. Arnoldi's Method

The natural basis $\{r_0, A r_0, A^2 r_0, \dots\}$ is notoriously ill-conditioned in practice (by the power iteration method, $A^k r_0$ rapidly aligns with the eigenvector of the dominant eigenvalue, becoming nearly parallel).
**Arnoldi's method** constructs an **orthonormal basis** $V_m = [v_1, v_2, \dots, v_m]$ for $\mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$ using the Modified Gram-Schmidt process.

### 2.1 The Arnoldi Algorithm
```
Algorithm: Arnoldi's Modified Gram-Schmidt Procedure
Input: Matrix A, initial vector r_0, dimension m
Output: Orthonormal basis V_m = [v_1, ..., v_m], upper Hessenberg matrix H_m

1. Initialize:
   β = norm(r_0, 2)
   if β == 0 then return x_0 end if
   v_1 = r_0 / β

2. For j = 1 to m do:
       w = A * v_j                      // Matrix-vector product (SpMV)
       for i = 1 to j do
           h[i, j] = v_i^T * w          // Orthogonalization coefficients
           w = w - h[i, j] * v_i        // Modified Gram-Schmidt subtraction
       end for
       h[j+1, j] = norm(w, 2)           // Subdiagonal entry
       
       if h[j+1, j] == 0 then
           Breakdown at step j!         // Invariant subspace found!
           return
       end if
       
       v_{j+1} = w / h[j+1, j]          // Normalize new basis vector
   end for
```

### 2.2 The Hessenberg Matrix
The orthogonalization coefficients form an **Upper Hessenberg matrix** $\bar{H}_m \in \mathbb{R}^{(m+1) \times m}$:
$$\bar{H}_m = \begin{bmatrix} h_{11} & h_{12} & h_{13} & \dots & h_{1m} \\ h_{21} & h_{22} & h_{23} & \dots & h_{2m} \\ 0 & h_{32} & h_{33} & \dots & h_{3m} \\ 0 & 0 & h_{43} & \dots & h_{4m} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & h_{m, m-1} & h_{mm} \\ 0 & 0 & \dots & 0 & h_{m+1, m} \end{bmatrix}$$
Deleting the last row of $\bar{H}_m$ yields the square $m \times m$ upper Hessenberg matrix $H_m$.

### 2.3 The Fundamental Arnoldi Relations
From the algorithm, at step $j$:
$$A v_j = \sum_{i=1}^{j+1} h_{ij} v_i$$
Collecting these columns for $j = 1, 2, \dots, m$ yields the **First Fundamental Arnoldi Relation**:
$$\mathbf{A V_m = V_m H_m + h_{m+1, m} v_{m+1} e_m^T = V_{m+1} \bar{H}_m}$$
where $e_m = [0, 0, \dots, 0, 1]^T \in \mathbb{R}^m$.

Multiply from the left by $V_m^T$. Since the columns of $V_m$ are orthonormal ($V_m^T V_m = I_m$) and $V_m^T v_{m+1} = \mathbf{0}$:
$$\mathbf{V_m^T A V_m = H_m}$$
$H_m$ is the **orthogonal projection of operator $A$ onto the Krylov subspace $\mathcal{K}_m$**!

### 2.4 The "Lucky Breakdown"
If $h_{j+1, j} = 0$ at step $j = m$:
$$A V_m = V_m H_m$$
This implies $\mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$ is strictly invariant under $A$. The Krylov subspace contains the exact solution $x^*$, and the algorithm terminates early with zero residual error!

## 3. Full Orthogonalization Method (FOM)

The **Full Orthogonalization Method (FOM)** is an orthogonal projection (Galerkin) method for general linear systems $Ax = b$ using Arnoldi's basis.

### 3.1 Mathematical Formulation
- Search subspace: $\mathcal{K}_m = \mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$
- Constraint subspace: $\mathcal{L}_m = \mathcal{K}_m(A, r_0)$ (Galerkin condition: $r_m \perp \mathcal{K}_m$)

Express candidate solution:
$$x_m = x_0 + V_m y_m$$
Enforce orthogonality of residual $r_m = r_0 - A V_m y_m$ to $V_m$:
$$V_m^T (r_0 - A V_m y_m) = \mathbf{0}$$
Substitute $r_0 = \beta v_1 = \beta V_m e_1$ and $V_m^T A V_m = H_m$:
$$V_m^T (\beta V_m e_1) - (V_m^T A V_m) y_m = \mathbf{0}$$
$$\beta I_m e_1 - H_m y_m = \mathbf{0}$$
$$\mathbf{H_m y_m = \beta e_1}$$
Assuming $H_m$ is invertible:
$$\mathbf{y_m = H_m^{-1} (\beta e_1)}$$
The approximate solution is:
$$\mathbf{x_m = x_0 + V_m H_m^{-1} (\beta e_1)}$$

### 3.2 Computational Characteristics and Issues with FOM
- **Linear Solve**: The original massive $n \times n$ problem is converted to an $m \times m$ upper Hessenberg solve ($H_m y_m = \beta e_1$), solved in $O(m^2)$ operations via Gaussian elimination without pivoting.
- **Potential Failure**: Even if $A$ is non-singular, the projected matrix $H_m$ can occasionally be singular, causing FOM to break down ($H_m^{-1}$ does not exist).
- **Residual Behavior**: Residual norm does not decrease monotonically; it can exhibit sharp spikes. This directly motivates **GMRES**.

## 4. The Symmetric Lanczos Algorithm

When matrix $A$ is **symmetric** ($A = A^T$), Arnoldi’s method undergoes a dramatic simplification that eliminates long-term memory requirements!

### 4.1 Symmetry of the Projected Matrix
From the second Arnoldi relation:
$$H_m = V_m^T A V_m$$
Take the transpose of $H_m$:
$$H_m^T = (V_m^T A V_m)^T = V_m^T A^T V_m = V_m^T A V_m = H_m$$
$H_m$ is **both upper Hessenberg and symmetric**!
- Being upper Hessenberg implies: $h_{ij} = 0$ for $i > j + 1$.
- Being symmetric implies: $h_{ij} = h_{ji}$, so $h_{ij} = 0$ for $j > i + 1$.
**Fundamental Theorem**: $H_m$ has non-zero entries **only on the main diagonal and the immediately adjacent sub- and super-diagonals**. It is a **TRIDIAGONAL MATRIX**, denoted $T_m$!

$$T_m = \begin{bmatrix} \alpha_1 & \beta_1 & & & \\ \beta_1 & \alpha_2 & \beta_2 & & \\ & \beta_2 & \alpha_3 & \ddots & \\ & & \ddots & \ddots & \beta_{m-1} \\ & & & \beta_{m-1} & \alpha_m \end{bmatrix}$$

### 4.2 The 3-Term Lanczos Recurrence
Because $T_m$ is tridiagonal, the column relation $A v_j = \sum_{i=1}^{j+1} h_{ij} v_i$ collapses to only **three terms**:
$$A v_j = \beta_{j-1} v_{j-1} + \alpha_j v_j + \beta_j v_{j+1}$$
Rearranging to solve for the next basis vector $v_{j+1}$:
$$\mathbf{\beta_j v_{j+1} = A v_j - \alpha_j v_j - \beta_{j-1} v_{j-1}}$$
where:
$$\mathbf{\alpha_j = v_j^T A v_j}$$
$$\mathbf{\beta_j = \|A v_j - \alpha_j v_j - \beta_{j-1} v_{j-1}\|_2}$$

### 4.3 The Symmetric Lanczos Algorithm
```
Algorithm: Symmetric Lanczos Procedure
Input: Symmetric matrix A, vector r_0
Output: Tridiagonal matrix T_m, Lanczos vectors v_j

1. β_0 = 0, v_0 = 0
   β = norm(r_0, 2)
   v_1 = r_0 / β

2. For j = 1 to m do:
       w = A * v_j - β_{j-1} * v_{j-1}
       α_j = v_j^T * w
       w = w - α_j * v_j
       β_j = norm(w, 2)
       if β_j == 0 then break end if
       v_{j+1} = w / β_j
   end for
```

### 4.4 The Direct Lanczos (D-Lanczos) Method for Linear Systems
For symmetric systems, FOM solves:
$$T_m y_m = \beta e_1$$
Because $T_m$ is tridiagonal, it can be factored efficiently using an $L D L^T$ factorization without pivoting (using the Thomas algorithm / TDMA).
- **Avoidance of Basis Vector Storage**:
  By factoring $T_m$ progressively, the approximate solution $x_m$ can be updated recursively from $x_{m-1}$ using auxiliary search directions $p_m$:
  $$x_m = x_{m-1} + \zeta_m p_m$$
  This allows solving symmetric linear systems **without storing previous basis vectors $\{v_1, \dots, v_{m-1}\}$ in memory**!
- This formulation is algebraically identical to the celebrated **Conjugate Gradient (CG) Method**.
